# Silver: everything has a price, and you know it

**Brainstorm only.** Nothing here is decided. See [the brainstorm index](README.md).

Other characters spend resources. A Silver converts them, insures them and trades them across categories that are normally separate: money, time, favors, information and risk.

**GM question:** What would they trade for it?

## Out of combat

- **Appraise.** Ask the GM "What is the price of this?" for anything: a guard's silence, a vote, a Gold's loyalty. The GM answers honestly, and the price is rarely money: "His silence costs a place for his daughter at the Academy."
- **The Ledger.** Your character sheet is a ledger of who owes whom and how much. Credits are power you can spend: call in a debt and a dockmaster opens a bay. Debits are liabilities the GM can call in at a bad moment. Debts can be sold, given to teammates or bundled: "I own three of the Governor's creditors, so now I own the Governor."
- **Underwriting.** This works like insurance. Before an operation you write policies: "If anyone falls in the duel, a surgeon is already paid and waiting." When the bad thing happens, the policy pays and turns a disaster into a cost. Premiums cost resources, and you have to predict what will go wrong.
- **Sealed Positions.** Before a session, write predictions in secret and seal them: "The envoy betrays us before the summit." If it happens, open it ("I sold his House's stock short a week ago") and collect a large payout. Flashbacks look back. This looks forward, but stays hidden until it pays off.
- **Exchange.** Convert one resource into another at a rate: supplies into time, Glory into money, information into progress on a clock. Every trade has a fee, and the rate depends on the situation. Ships are expensive in wartime.
- **Read the Market.** Prices move before the news does. If a House is quietly buying grain and medicine, they expect a siege. The GM gives you price signals and you work out the secret, which is a puzzle style of investigation no other Color has.
- **Investments.** Between sessions, put resources into a venture or a person, such as funding a young Blue's training. They pay off later, or not at all. The Blue you sponsored might be your fleet captain in the final arc.

## In combat

- **Make an Offer.** Partway through a fight, offer an enemy more than they're paid. Golds rarely sell, but their hired Grays might. You're attacking their contract instead of their armor.
- **Contracts.** Call in mercenaries you hired earlier. They fight well but have terms: they leave if they're unpaid, if the fight turns against them, or if someone offers more. You're a summoner whose summons can be outbid.
- **Already Paid For.** Declare that a delivery arrives this round: armor, ammunition, a gunship. It uses resources you set aside earlier.
- **Cut the Supply.** If you bought the enemy's supplier last week, their pulseFists run dry in round three.
- **Bet on It.** Wager on a teammate's roll or a duel. This gives you a stake in every roll, and a constant temptation to cheat the outcome.

## Weaknesses

- You can't buy people who want nothing you have: zealots, a Red who wants only revenge, a Gold whose pride is the price.
- Money attracts thieves, rivals and auditors.
- Debts run both ways.
- A market crash can wipe out your wealth in one session.

## Example: the duel

Your Gold has to duel a rival.

- You appraise the rival's second. His price is his gambling debt cleared, so you buy that debt and own him.
- You take bets across the crowd at long odds on your Gold, and you underwrite a surgeon.
- Your Gold is wounded in the first exchange, and the surgeon is already there.
- The rival's razor turns out to be poorly maintained.
- Your winnings fund the next operation.

## Developed further

### Underwriting

Before an operation, write up to three policy cards.

| Card part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Insured | Our Gold |
| Trigger | He falls in the duel |
| Payout | A surgeon is already paid and waiting |
| Premium | 2 resources, paid now |

- **Payouts** can be a prepared response (a surgeon, an extraction team, a bribe already in place), resources, or a softer consequence when a danger clock fills.
- **The premium tells you something.** The GM quotes the price, and in the story, insurers price real risk. If the market charges a fortune to insure the envoy's safety, someone knows something. Underwriting becomes a way to gather information.
- **Your crew takes bigger risks.** "Go ahead, you're covered" becomes part of the crew's tactics.
- **Fraud.** You can cause a trigger on purpose to collect. If you're caught, insurers stop dealing with you.
- **Hedging.** Sell policies to NPCs, or insure both sides of a duel. Now you have a stake in outcomes that don't involve you.
- **In operations:** "If the steward's suspicion clock fills, a forged transfer order pulls him away from the gala."

### Offers

Partway through a fight, make an offer to an enemy group or person. The GM tells you what kind of loyalty they have.

| Loyalty | Can you buy them? |
| --- | --- |
| **Bought** (paid soldiers) | Yes, if you beat their pay plus the risk of switching |
| **Afraid** (coerced) | Yes, if you offer protection along with the money |
| **Sworn** (bound by an oath) | Only after a White undoes the oath |
| **Believer** (zealots) | No |

- **Results:** they switch sides, stand down, retreat, or hesitate for a round while they think it over.
- **Even a refused offer costs them.** Their officers have to spend actions keeping the troops in line. You're fighting their morale and their contracts.
- **A lowball offer insults them** and makes them harder to buy.
- **You have to pay.** If you don't, word spreads and your next offer is worth less.

### Contracts

Hire mercenaries ahead of time and write their contract on a card: pay, duration, duties, exclusions ("we don't fight Obsidians") and exit terms.

- **The GM plays them by the letter of the contract.** They do exactly what it says, so writing tight clauses matters as much as any roll, and the GM gets to find the loopholes.
- **Outbidding.** Enemies can try to buy your mercenaries. Clauses like "half the pay comes at the end" or "exclusive to us" protect you.
- **Sabotage.** If you know who the enemy hired, buy that contract, or slip a clause in through the broker: "In case of fire, they withdraw." Then start a fire.
- **You command without fighting.** Mercenaries act on your turn, so you play like a general who never holds a weapon.

## Combos with other Colors

- **White:** a contract blessed by a White can't be outbid by anyone honorable. A Silver finds a witness's price, and the White makes their testimony binding. See [White](white.md).
- **Copper:** a contract a Copper files can be enforced by the courts. See [Copper](copper.md).
- **Pink:** a Pink reads which enemy is about to betray their side, which tells you exactly who to make an offer to. See [Brown and Pink](brown-and-pink.md).
