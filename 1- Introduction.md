The Red Rising Tabletop RPG (R2T2) is designed to provide the framework for a party of players to play out campaigns across the each of the spheres of the Solar System and the all space between. Players are given the opportunity to play as any of the fourteen Colors of the Society, each with their own strengths, weakness, skills, and flavor. 

When the mankind first began colonizing the worlds of the Solar system and built the Color system, it was under the belief that every person needed a role to fill for the group as a whole to survive the rigors and danger of space exploration. While the Society eventually twisted the system itself into a machine to support Gold domination rather than guidance, the underlying belief that every character has an important role to play to further the goals of the party should be felt throughout the design of this game.

## Setting

This Handbook is set in the period after the end of Morning Star, at the start of the Solar War in 744 PCE. Accordingly, characters, locations, alliances, governments, and technology set at this time period may be seen (like the Societal Remnant controlling Mercury), while others (like colonized Rhea or Lorn au Arcos) won’t. Campaigns in different time periods or locations may exclude some contents of this Handbook or add new features to fit the setting. 

## Core Game Mechanics

Anyone familiar with TTRPGs is likely to recognize many of the general game mechanics, though all of the game mechanics have been built from the ground up to suit the specific intricacies of the Red Rising universe. These are not mere reskins of game mechanics from elsewhere— each is tailored to fit the canon and lore of the universe.

#### Character Creation

*[[0- Colors|Colors]]* take the place of races in other games. Though the 14 Colors are all human at their core, the difference in an Obsidian Stained and a Pink Rose will often differ more in physiology and play-style than would a half-orc and a halfling in other games. *No illusion of Color parity is assumed in this game*. While every Color has their own collection of strengths and weaknesses, a Red is not beating an Obsidian in an arm wrestling match, a Brown is not out-sprinting a Gold, and a Yellow isn’t beating a Pink in a beauty pageant. A major theme of this game is learning to apply your Color’s strengths effectively and minimizing their shortcomings.

*[[Classes]]* are similar to what you would find elsewhere but have some caveats. Not every Color choose every class, and in fact, most classes are limited to a few select Colors. In some cases, only one Color can choose a specific class. A White can’t wake up at the beginning of a campaign and choose to be a Helldiver after all.   

*[[Ability Scores|Aspects]]* are a new take on ability scores. A player has three Aspects that describe their skills in different domains: Physical, Social, and Mental. Each of those Aspects are subdivided into 4 abilities. Those abilities are:

Physical: Strength, Dexterity, Agility, Constitution
Social: Charisma, Guile, Loyalty, Caste
Mental: Intelligence, Wisdom, Courage, Perception

This split allows for a granular description of what a character has skill in. Moving fast (Agility) is different than being skillful with your hands (Dexterity). This is also used to give players different actions in a turn so that every turn isn’t a decision between utility and combat.

# Gameplay

Speaking broadly, gameplay is separated into 4 types of play:

Downtime- where players are not specifically working toward any specific objective or goal. This is often going to be time in their quarters, shopping, healing, or generally between other activities.

Exploration- where players are investigating, talking to NPCs, gathering intel, accepting quests, and, well, exploring.

Open Conflict- turn- and initiative-based combat or other conflict of incidental or casual nature or that generally is not expected to require major coordination for success. Ex. You get in a fight at a bar. You run into a guard when you’re somewhere you shouldn’t be. You get attacked by a gang. A boulder is rolling behind you down a tunnel.

[[1- Introduction#Scenes|Scenes]]- these are either large scale operations or conflicts of particular significance. This could be as small as a negotiation between the party and another faction or as big as an Iron Rain. The Scene will be divided up into multiple *Fronts* and different party members may take part or be assigned to completing tasks in a specific Front. Success or failure in a Front may advance or hinder progression of a [[1- Introduction#Clocks|Clock]] and may affect activities in other Fronts. In general, your goal will be to fill your Scene Clock before the opposition does. Teamwork is *paramount* in Scenes. Rarely, if ever, will a single character succeed or fail a Scene on their on individual actions.

# Clocks

Progress toward objectives is tracked using graphical representations called Clocks. These consist of a circle partitioned into equal slices (like a pie) and each section is filled in as a relevant objective or action is completed. When a Clock is completely filled in, the overall mission, goal, or event that it is representing is completed.

These are meant to help keep gameplay clear, transparent, and motivating for players and the GM alike. The party may have many different clocks that are simultaneously being tracked at any given time. Some-- like breaking into a vault-- may be immediately relevant and filled quickly while others-- like solving a major mystery or defeating a faction-- may slowly fill over the course of the entire campaign. 

Clocks can also be used to visualize conflict in a couple of ways:

Competing Clocks: where two sides (which may or may not include the party) are both competing to fill their own Clock first. An example: the party is trying to escape from a facility with a stolen drive, while a group of guards are trying to catch them. Clearing locked doors or disabling security cameras may advance the party's Clock while activating alarms and identifying the party's escape route might advance the opposition Clock.

Tug-of-War Clocks: where two sides have the same or similar goal and are actively trying to execute on it at the same time. This is an especially useful version when the scenario is "zero-sum", or, in other words, every advantage one side gets is at the detriment of the other. An example of this might be two factions trying to influence a single government official to ruling in their favor. These scenarios may not even have the party as a member of the clock itself, but they may still try to influence the balance of the clock by providing intel, intimidation, or by creating distractions to influence the outcome. When using ToW Clocks, part of the Clock should start filled in (representing one side) and the other should be empty (representing the opposition). If the Clock becomes totally filled in, the former side wins. If it ends up totally empty, the latter side wins. These Clocks do *not* have to start balanced at the start.

Timer Clocks: where an event will occur after a number of specified turns, actions, or events have occurred. An example would be escaping from a soon-to-be destroyed ship where every subsequent attack on the ship furthers the progression to the ship's-- and party's-- ultimate demise.

# Scenes

Scenes are structured, multi-faceted encounters used for significant combat and non-combat encounters. Scenes are divided into different Fronts which serve as varied forms of engagement within the scene. In general, Scenes are divided into 3 main kinds of Fronts: Physical fronts, Social Fronts, and Mental fronts. While the Fronts are divided into these three general groups, there is no limit to the number of Fronts a scene may present.

Each Front will have objectives, or a series of objectives, that participants on that Front are trying to accomplish. These will be tracked by Clocks. When a character or group successfully completes a Front Clock, it will move the entire party closer to completion of the Scene Clock— which tracks completion of the goal the entire party is attempting to achieve. Completion of some Front Clocks can affect the ease of completion of objectives on other Fronts as well. But be warned! Your opposition has its own machinations. If a party member fails on their objective on a Front, an Opposition Front Clock may be completed instead or you may incur Complications— negative results that impair your progress.
